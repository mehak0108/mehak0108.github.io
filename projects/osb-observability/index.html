OSB Observability Layer — Proactive Fault Detection & Alerting

Designed and deployed a two-layer observability system across JPMC's internal service marketplace — synthetic canary testing plus conditional alert routing — cutting fault detection time by 97% and eliminating the cross-team war rooms that preceded every incident.

Problem

JPMC's internal service marketplace runs on the Open Service Broker (OSB) protocol, with ~20 internal teams exposing services across 6 datacenter pools. When a Create, Bind, Unbind, or Delete operation failed, the platform had no way to tell whether the fault was in the Gaia platform itself or in a specific provider's OSB implementation. Every failure escalated into a cross-team war room, with engineers spending 60–90 minutes just agreeing on fault domain before anyone started fixing anything.

Detection was entirely reactive — failures surfaced only when a developer hit them and filed a ticket, often hours after the fault began. Pool-specific failures were nearly invisible: a service that worked in one pool but failed in another looked like a non-reproducible, intermittent bug. Alert channels were shared across all 20 providers, so teams couldn't distinguish their own failures from someone else's — leading to alert fatigue and missed critical signals. Leadership had no way to assess marketplace health without manually correlating incident tickets.

Solution

Discovery across service providers, platform engineering, and Service Fabric leadership surfaced the root gap: no synthetic testing existed anywhere in the OSB request path, so every failure signal came from real developer traffic. I evaluated four alternatives — enhanced logging, provider self-monitoring, stricter ticketing SLAs, and canary testing without routing — and rejected all of them as either still-reactive or noise-generating at scale.

The solution was a two-layer system, since neither layer alone was sufficient:

  • Canary test suite — continuously exercises all four OSB operations against every provider's endpoint in every pool, on a 5-minute cadence, isolated from real developer traffic. At 20 providers × 6 pools × 4 operations, this generates ~138,000 automated test executions per day. Because the canary runs an identical test service alongside each provider's, it definitively separates platform-side faults from provider-side faults — if only one provider fails while everyone else passes, the fault is theirs.
  • GTF Rules Engine integration — rather than building a new alerting system, I integrated with JPMC's existing Rules Engine to route canary results with conditional logic: provider-scoped alerts only reach the responsible team, platform-side faults route only to platform engineering, a flap filter requires two consecutive failures before alerting, and recovery notifications close the loop automatically.
  • Grafana dashboard — a live health matrix serving both providers (their own 4×6 operation/pool grid) and Service Fabric leadership (all 20 providers at a glance), replacing what used to require an incident ticket or a call to assess.

Outcome

Mean time to detection dropped from 2–8 hours to ~5 minutes — failures are now caught within one canary cycle, at any hour, before a developer is affected. Mean time to resolution fell from 3–5 hours to ~60 minutes, driven almost entirely by eliminating the fault-attribution phase. Cross-team incident calls dropped from ~4/week to ~1/week, with the residual calls being genuine shared-infrastructure issues rather than routine attribution investigations. An estimated 650+ engineer-hours per year were reclaimed from triage, and for the first time, all 6 pools had live health coverage with an objective accountability view for OSB implementation quality across all 20 provider teams.
