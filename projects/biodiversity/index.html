Led a 5-person team merging NPS biodiversity, visitation, and funding datasets across 53 national parks into a single Ecological Stress Index, revealing that federal conservation dollars track park popularity far more than ecological need, then translated the findings into three funding-policy recommendations.

Problem

The National Park Service has to do two things that pull against each other: protect fragile ecosystems and welcome the public into them. Every visitor adds wear — eroded trails, disrupted wildlife, more invasive species hitchhiking in on boots and gear — and federal funding is supposed to scale with that pressure. According to Buxton et al. (2020), U.S. spending on endangered species recovery sits at only 25% of what full implementation actually requires, and that shortfall doesn't land evenly. Some parks absorb far more ecological strain than their budgets can support; others draw outsized funding relative to the conservation burden they actually carry.

We had access to park-level NPS data on species inventory, visitation, acreage, and funding, but no single number that captured which parks were actually under the most ecological stress. Our task: build one, and test whether the current funding model lines up with it.

Solution

We merged two messy Kaggle datasets (species inventory and visitation) with manually transcribed NPS Budget Book figures, fixing structural issues along the way, comma-separated values that had shifted entire columns, mismatched park names, inconsistent encodings, then calculated park-level metrics like funding per visitor, visitors per acre, and percent non-native species.

Four targeted analyses built toward the final model. First, we found a stark resource-versus-impact mismatch: remote Alaskan parks like Gates of the Arctic get $423.80 in funding per visitor, while Great Smoky Mountains, which holds over 2,100 species, operates on just $1.58. Second, using conditional SQL aggregation across native-vs-non-native species classifications, we confirmed that the top 10% most-visited parks have a native-to-invasive ratio of just 7.32:1, compared to 23.89:1 in the bottom 10% — direct evidence that foot traffic itself accelerates invasive species introduction. Third, a funding-per-at-risk-species analysis showed Great Basin (96 at-risk species) receiving $36,250 per species, while Denali (31 at-risk species, but a $1.9M/year park road) receives $531,613 — funding tracks infrastructure and popularity, not biodiversity threat. Fourth, we built a weighted concern score that separated genuinely endangered species from lower-risk categories and isolated seven parks (Death Valley, Redwood, Channel Islands, Hawaii Volcanoes, Everglades, Haleakala, Biscayne) carrying disproportionate ecological burden tied to pollution, invasive species, or climate exposure.

We then combined all three pressure dimensions, funding imbalance, visitor density, and invasive species ratio, into a single Ecological Stress Index: (Visitor Rank − Funding Rank) + (Visitors Per Acre × 0.25) + (Invasive Species Ratio × 100). Plotting ESI scores against at-risk species counts across all 53 parks showed a clear positive relationship, confirming that ecological vulnerability isn't random; it's driven by the interaction of crowding, funding, and invasive pressure.

Outcome

The analysis produced a single diagnostic score, the Ecological Stress Index, capable of flagging which parks need conservation intervention most urgently, independent of how popular or well-funded they appear on paper. It surfaced a structural problem: funding rank barely correlates with conservation-need rank across the system. From there we issued three policy recommendations directly tied to the data: reform the federal funding formula to weight biodiversity need alongside infrastructure and visitation, reinstate timed-entry visitor caps at the highest-stress parks (Acadia, Zion, Great Smoky Mountains), and restore ranger and science staffing, which has dropped nearly 25% since January 2025 even as visitation and at-risk species counts climb.
