Designed and led delivery of JP Morgan Resource Naming (JRN), a structured naming standard enforced via platform gates across Gaia Cloud Foundry and Kubernetes — fixing workload cost attribution for 80+ teams and turning a 22-day manual billing cycle into 4 days.

Problem Statement

Organizational Context

JP Morgan Chase's Global Technology Infrastructure (GTI) line of business operates JPMC's internal private cloud platforms, Gaia Cloud Foundry (CF) and the Gaia Kubernetes Platform (GKP), which together serve as the primary deployment targets for application workloads across all major business lines: Corporate & Investment Bank (CIB), Commercial Banking, Asset & Wealth Management, Consumer & Community Banking, and Enterprise Technology. At the time, GTI supported over 80 independent technology teams deploying and running an estimated 12,000+ active workloads (CF applications, GKP deployments, and associated services) across these platforms.

Platform resource consumption, compute (RAM and vCPU allocations), storage, and network bandwidth, was metered by the Gaia platform and invoiced internally to GTI's Finance business partner on a monthly cycle. GTI Finance was then responsible for allocating those platform costs back to the correct LOB division through the firm's internal chargeback process.

The Attribution Breakdown

The internal chargeback process depended on a critical assumption: that each workload on Gaia could be reliably linked to an owning LOB, division, and team. In practice, this assumption did not hold. Across the GTI estate, approximately 40% of workloads, running live on Gaia CF and GKP, carried no structured ownership metadata. Application names like "payment-svc", "data-processor-v2", or "test-app-1" were common, containing no reliable signal about which business unit had provisioned the workload.

The consequences were significant and affected multiple stakeholder groups:

  • GTI Finance and billing teams were manually cross-referencing workload lists against ServiceNow provisioning tickets and team rosters, a reconciliation process that required 3–4 analysts and averaged 22 business days per monthly billing cycle.
  • Division finance leads were receiving cost allocations that were routinely challenged, creating friction in month-end close and escalations to GTI leadership.
  • GTI platform engineering had no reliable inventory of what was running on Gaia, who owned it, or whether it was production-critical, a risk exposure for platform capacity planning and incident response.
  • Security Operations could not quickly identify the owning team for a workload exhibiting anomalous behavior, increasing mean-time-to-triage for platform-level security incidents.

Discovery & Stakeholder Alignment

How the Problem Was Surfaced

The issue was escalated by GTI Finance following a quarterly close review in which over $950K in Gaia platform charges could not be automatically attributed to a cost center. I was brought in as the technical representative from the GTI platform team to lead root cause analysis and solution design.

I conducted structured discovery interviews across four stakeholder groups to map the full problem space before proposing any solution:

  • Finance & Billing teams — to understand exactly what metadata they needed in order to execute chargeback: the minimum viable fields, acceptable formats, and what a machine-readable output would look like.
  • GTI Tech Leads (sample of 14 teams across 4 divisions) — to understand current provisioning behaviors on Gaia CF and GKP, what naming conventions existed (if any), and what constraints any solution would face in their CI/CD pipelines.
  • Gaia Platform Engineering — to understand what enforcement hooks existed at the CF API layer and within GKP's admission control pipeline, and what metadata was already captured at deploy time.
  • Internal Solution Architects — to evaluate feasibility of technical approaches and identify dependencies on ServiceNow, the internal CMDB, and existing CI/CD tooling.

Key Insights from Discovery

  • No consistent naming convention existed across GTI teams. Each team used ad-hoc names for CF apps and GKP workloads with no shared schema.
  • CF org and space structures were used for environment isolation (prod, staging, dev) but did not map reliably to billing divisions, a single CF space frequently contained apps owned by multiple cost centers.
  • GKP namespaces were similarly used as operational constructs (one namespace per team or service family), not financial ones, and namespace names did not encode division or LOB.
  • Kubernetes labels were applied inconsistently: roughly 25% of GKP workloads had a team label, but with no agreed schema, values like team-a, payments, and CIB-Payments all appeared in the same platform.
  • Finance's minimum data requirement for chargeback was: LOB identifier, division, and owning team, embedded in a machine-parseable, programmatically extractable format.
  • The Gaia platform already emitted per-workload consumption metrics (RAM allocation, vCPU hours, storage); the gap was purely in linking those metrics to an owner.

The evaluation showed that all the alternatives either failed to address the existing unattributed workload inventory, relied on voluntary team compliance (which had already failed), or required disruptive structural changes to the Gaia platform itself. The gap pointed to a different class of solution: a naming-based standard where ownership metadata is embedded in the workload name itself at provisioning time, parseable by the billing pipeline without any additional integrations, and enforceable natively through existing platform hooks.

Solution: JP Morgan Resource Naming (JRN)

Design Principles

Inspired by Amazon Resource Naming (ARN), the structured naming convention AWS uses to uniquely identify all resources in its ecosystem, JRN was designed for JPMC's private cloud context around four principles:

  • Self-describing: the workload name alone encodes LOB, division, team, environment, and resource type, no lookup in any external system required.
  • Platform-agnostic: the same schema works across Gaia Cloud Foundry apps, GKP deployments, and any future internal or external cloud platform JPMC adopts.
  • Machine-parseable: a fixed delimiter and fixed-position segment structure enables regex-based extraction in billing pipelines, monitoring dashboards, and policy engines.
  • Enforced at the platform gate: not a recommendation, a hard blocker. Non-compliant workloads cannot be deployed.

The JRN Schema

After three design review cycles with the solution architect team and validation sessions with Finance on the required chargeback fields, the following schema was finalized:

jpmc:<lob>:<division>:<team>:<environment>:<datacenter>:<platform>:<workload-id>

CF example: jpmc:gti:cib:payments-platform:prod:ny4:cf-app:trade-confirm-svc GKP example: jpmc:gti:cib:payments-platform:prod:ny4:k8s-deploy:trade-confirm-worker

Rationale for each schema segment:

  • <lob> — Line of Business (e.g., gti, cib, ccb). Top-level allocation unit matching JPMC's financial reporting hierarchy.
  • <division> — Business division within the LOB (e.g., cib, awm, commercial-banking). The primary chargeback unit requested by Finance for internal billing.
  • <team> — Provisioning tech team (e.g., payments-platform, market-data-infra). Enables granular cost visibility per team and immediate ownership identification for security triage.
  • <environment> — prod, uat, staging, dev, sandbox. Allows platform policies to enforce environment-specific controls (e.g., prevent sandbox workloads from being tagged as prod to avoid high-availability guarantees).
  • <datacenter> — Internal datacenter code (e.g., ny4, ny5, de2, tx1). Supports data residency compliance tracking and capacity planning by physical location.
  • <platform> — cf-app, cf-service, k8s-deploy, k8s-statefulset, k8s-cronjob, etc. Enables resource-class cost breakdowns and platform-specific operational tooling.
  • <workload-id> — Human-readable workload name (no spaces). Provides operational identity and retains team-defined naming conventions within the JRN structure.

Data Storage & Downstream Integration

The JRN itself required no separate registry or database, the resource name is the JRN, stored natively in Gaia Cloud Foundry's internal API and GKP's etcd store alongside every running workload. The billing pipeline queried these platform APIs on a nightly schedule, extracted all active resource names, and applied a regex parser to pull out the LOB, division, team, environment, and datacenter segments for cost attribution.

Two lightweight auxiliary stores were maintained alongside:

  • JRN Reference Table — a curated list of approved segment values (valid LOB codes, division identifiers, team slugs), maintained by the GTI Architecture Council. The CF API hook and GKP admission webhook validated incoming resource names against this table at deploy time, preventing typos or unrecognized team identifiers from silently slipping through into the billing pipeline.
  • Team-to-Cost-Center Mapping — Finance's chargeback system operates on internal cost center codes (e.g., CC-47821), not team slugs. A mapping table linking JRN team identifiers to their corresponding cost center codes was maintained in ServiceNow, giving the billing pipeline a reliable key to translate parsed JRN segments into the financial hierarchy without any manual intervention.

Platform Enforcement

JRN compliance was enforced through two complementary platform-level gates — one for each Gaia platform, so that a non-compliant workload name causes a hard deployment failure rather than a policy warning:

  • Gaia Cloud Foundry — CF API pre-provisioning hook: A validation hook was implemented at the CF API layer that intercepts every cf push command. Before the application is staged, the hook validates the application name against the JRN regex. A non-matching name returns an error with the expected format and rejects the push. This was implemented in collaboration with the Gaia CF platform engineering team and deployed as a CF API plugin at the foundation level, requiring no changes to team-level cf push workflows beyond the name format.
  • Gaia Kubernetes Platform — Validating Admission Webhook: A ValidatingAdmissionWebhook was deployed to the GKP cluster that intercepts all Deployment, StatefulSet, CronJob, and DaemonSet creation requests. The webhook validates the resource's metadata.name field against the JRN regex and rejects any resource that does not conform. The webhook was registered at the cluster level by the GKP platform team, with zero changes required to individual team Helm charts beyond the workload name.
  • CI/CD pipeline linting (defense-in-depth): A JRN linting step was added to JPMC's shared CI pipeline templates (used by 60+ teams as the base for their pipelines). This catches non-compliant names earlier in the development cycle, at the pull request stage, before a deployment is even attempted, reducing friction caused by late-stage rejections.

Retroactive Migration

The enforcement gates addressed net-new workloads, but approximately 4,800 existing workloads on Gaia were already deployed with non-compliant names. A separate migration workstream was run in parallel:

  • An automated inventory script queried the Gaia CF API and GKP API to extract all running workloads and their current names, cross-referenced against the ServiceNow provisioning records and team rosters to infer likely ownership.
  • A pre-populated migration spreadsheet was generated for each GTI tech team, showing their inferred workload ownership and requesting confirmation or correction. Teams had 3 weeks to validate.
  • Validated workloads were renamed programmatically using the CF API (cf rename) and Kubernetes resource patching scripts. For CF apps, this triggered an automatic restage; for GKP, workloads were redeployed via the team's CI pipeline with the new name.
  • At the end of the migration, 4,680 of 4,800 existing workloads (97.5%) were successfully migrated. The remaining 120 were legacy, ownerless workloads that were subsequently decommissioned after a 30-day notice period — an unanticipated side benefit of the exercise.

Impact & Results

Internal Chargeback & Financial Accuracy

  • $250M+ in previously unattributed annual Gaia platform charges correctly allocated to the owning division, enabling accurate financial reporting and significantly reducing escalations at quarterly close.
  • Billing cycle resolution time reduced from 22 business days to 4 business days, transforming cost allocation from a backward-looking reconciliation exercise to a near-real-time operational process.
  • Billing disputes per quarter dropped from ~52 to ~7 — a reduction of 87% — with residual disputes limited to legitimate edge cases around shared services workloads.
  • Manual reconciliation effort reduced from ~3.5 FTE per monthly cycle to less than 0.5 FTE, freeing Finance analysts for higher-value FinOps and cost optimization work.

Platform Operational Benefits

  • GTI gained a complete, live inventory of all workloads running on Gaia, by LOB, division, team, environment, datacenter, and platform type, for the first time. This supported capacity planning, rightsizing analysis, and platform cost optimization decisions.
  • The retroactive migration exercise surfaced 120 ownerless legacy workloads consuming live Gaia resources with no active team accountable for them. These were decommissioned, recovering an estimated $180K/year in idle platform capacity.
  • Gaia platform engineering was able to publish per-division capacity utilization reports to GTI leadership, enabling data-driven conversations about platform investment and quota allocation.

Security & Compliance

  • Security Operations gained immediate workload-to-team traceability for all Gaia workloads. Mean-time-to-triage for Gaia platform security incidents was reduced as the owning team could be identified directly from the workload name, without a manual CMDB lookup.
  • For regulatory and internal audit requests (OCC, internal risk reviews), any Gaia workload could be traced to its owning division and team in seconds rather than requiring a manual investigation spanning multiple systems.
  • Environment enforcement via the JRN schema enabled the Gaia platform team to apply environment-specific policies (high-availability SLAs, backup requirements, network segmentation rules) based on the <environment> segment of the JRN, a capability that did not exist before.

Foundation for Future Extensibility

  • Public cloud readiness: When JPMC began expanding workloads to public cloud, JRN's platform-agnostic schema was extended to cover resources on AWS and Azure without redesign, the same naming standard applied across the hybrid estate, giving Finance a unified attribution model from day one.
  • Golden path adoption: The JRN-compliant Helm chart templates and CF manifest templates became part of JPMC's internal platform engineering golden path, adopted by new teams as the standard starting point for Gaia deployments.
  • Developer experience: The shared CI linting module was adopted by teams beyond GTI, extending JRN compliance to application teams in other LOBs deploying on shared Gaia infrastructure.
