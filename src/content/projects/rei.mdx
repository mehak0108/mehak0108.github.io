## Problem

REI competes in a red ocean: the portable charging market is full of incremental players — Goal Zero, BioLite, Anker — fighting on watts, capacity, and price, while traditional rain flies, tarps, and backpack covers stay completely disconnected from that fight. Meanwhile every trend points the same direction: outdoor recreation participation sits near 164 million Americans, smartphone dependence is at 91%, and REI's own Co-op members increasingly expect sustainability built into the gear itself. Buyers who skip portable chargers altogether aren't rejecting the need for power — they're rejecting the bulk, the wiring, the multiple parts, and the e-waste that come with current solutions. No competitor had found a way to deliver power without asking customers to carry, learn, or maintain a separate device.

The brief: find a new market space REI could own outright, size it, and build a defensible case that it beats every realistic alternative.

## Solution

We ran the Blue Ocean toolkit end to end. A buyer utility map across six stages (purchase, delivery, use, supplements, maintenance, disposal) showed that every existing charging option failed buyers on simplicity and convenience, not just price. The Six Paths framework pointed us toward looking across complementary products — instead of competing in chargers, fuse the charger into gear customers already carry. That produced SolWeave: a thin-film solar fabric woven directly into an REI-branded rainfly tent cover, eliminating cords and rigid panels, raising sustainability and seamless integration, reducing pack weight and learning curve, and creating an entirely new category — solar-integrated outdoor fabric.

We stress-tested the idea against two alternatives — a fully integrated solar tent, and solar charging stations built into backcountry refugios — using a weighted criteria matrix (strategic fit, market opportunity, financial return, customer value, competitor risk, feasibility). The rainfly concept won decisively, scoring +6 against +2 for each alternative, driven by high-margin licensing potential and REI's existing supply chain and retail footprint absorbing most of the rollout cost.

We then sized the opportunity bottom-up: of ~164 million Americans in outdoor recreation, ~48 million are frequent campers/hikers, ~38.4 million of those carry a device outdoors, ~7.7 million are premium early adopters, and a 10% capture rate puts the addressable Year-1 market at 770,000 units — roughly $270M at a $350 price point. We ran Roger's Five Factors to anticipate adoption friction (trialability was the biggest risk, addressed with an in-store demo and 7-day rental program), priced the product at $249 against $370+ in comparable standalone value, and built a four-stage marketing funnel (awareness through social and Co-op email, consideration through in-store expert sessions, trial through rental and bundling, purchase through Co-op discounts) targeted at sustainability-minded, high-income, West Coast outdoor enthusiasts. A competitor-response model and three named risk mitigations (technological feasibility, tariffs, customer trust in REI as a new tech entrant) rounded out the plan.

## Outcome

The completed business case delivered a full Blue Ocean strategy and go-to-market plan for REI: a validated new product category scoring +6 against the next-best alternative's +2, a bottom-up market size of $270M in addressable Year-1 revenue from a 770,000-unit early-adopter target, pricing that captures roughly a third of estimated customer value, and a phased rollout plan from in-store demos through Co-op bundling — presented as a Blue Ocean Strategy Group Project for Paw Club Team 1 at the Foster School of Business.
