## Problem

A venue selling 500 seats across two waves, Premium first, then General Admission, faces a one-shot, irreversible decision: how many seats to protect for Premium buyers before GA sales open. Protect too few, and high-margin Premium demand gets turned away once GA fills the house. Protect too many, and seats sit empty that GA customers would gladly have bought. Premium demand is uncertain, GA demand is assumed to always be high enough to absorb whatever isn't protected, and once the protection number is set, it can't be revised. Get the number wrong in either direction, and the venue leaves real margin on the table.

## Solution

This is a textbook newsvendor problem, so I framed it that way directly: cost of overage was the $60 GA contribution margin lost on every seat protected but not sold to a Premium buyer, cost of underage was the $70 margin gap ($130 Premium minus $60 GA) lost on every Premium buyer turned away. That gives a critical ratio of 70 / (70 + 60) = 0.538, and against the given discrete demand distribution, the smallest protection level where cumulative probability meets or exceeds that ratio is 150 seats, at a 0.7 service level.

I validated this against a Monte Carlo simulator (built and hosted as an interactive tool) across five verification tests: confirming GA plus Premium plus empty seats always summed to exactly 500, checking the boundary cases of zero protection (all 500 go to GA) and full protection (capped by the maximum possible demand of 250, so 350 seats sit permanently empty), and comparing the simulator's expected contribution margin at P=150 ($37,870) against the hand-calculated value ($37,900) — a gap under 1%.

I then ran the model through three pricing-sensitivity scenarios. Raising the Premium price from $150 to $250 pushes the critical ratio to 0.739 and shifts optimal protection from 150 to 200 seats, exactly matching the simulator's profit-curve peak. A scenario introducing standing-room as a GA fallback option widened the Premium-GA margin gap, raising the critical ratio further while leaving the 150-seat optimum unchanged given the demand distribution. A salvage-value scenario, where late-released Premium seats can still recover most of the foregone GA margin, cut the effective cost of overage from $60 to $10, which rationally pushed the optimal protection level up to 200: when the downside of over-protecting nearly disappears, the model bets more aggressively on Premium demand materializing.

## Outcome

The analysis converged on a clear recommendation: protect 150 of 500 seats for Premium buyers in the baseline case, validated independently by both the analytical newsvendor solution and Monte Carlo simulation within 1% of each other, and confirmed robust across three pricing and inventory-recovery scenarios that each moved the optimal answer in the theoretically correct direction. The simulator also surfaced a real bug during verification, a rounding error in how empty seats were calculated and displayed, caught precisely because the visualization made the abstract overage cost tangible enough to notice something was off.
