## How the Problem Was Found

Product problems worth solving usually surface as weak signals before they become clear opportunities. The RFID self-checkout opportunity at MLSE followed exactly this pattern, a qualitative observation from the Customer Success team triggered a structured data investigation that built progressively from anecdote to a validated business case.

The discovery process moved through four sequential steps, each adding specificity and rigour to the initial signal: qualitative observation from the Customer Success team, session data analysis from Mishipay's logs, triangulated frustration evidence from external sources, and a bottom-up revenue model. Critically, the session data alone could not prove customer frustration, it could only show the throughput ceiling. A separate evidence layer was needed to establish that the ceiling was causing lost sales, not just limiting completed ones.

### What the Session Data Actually Showed

Mishipay's backend logs every checkout interaction as a structured session: start timestamp, payment-confirmed timestamp, payment status, item count, order value, payment method, and device ID. Pulling this data for MLSE across a rolling 90-day window revealed three clear signals:

- Average session duration (start to payment confirmed) was consistently ~90 seconds across completed transactions, this established the throughput baseline used in the revenue model
- Session volume on game days ran approximately 3× the weekday baseline, concentrated in two 15-minute windows per event matching the intermission schedule, confirming intermissions as the demand peak
- Session count per intermission hit a ceiling of approximately 30 transactions per window (900 sec ÷ 90 sec × 3 kiosks) and stayed there even on high-attendance sellout games. The session data wasn't showing low demand; it was showing suppressed demand. Customers who saw the queue and walked away never started a session, so they were invisible in the logs. The ceiling itself was the signal
- Average order value was higher on game days than off-peak, fans buying jerseys alongside additional items, meaning each transaction the ceiling was blocking represented disproportionate revenue loss, not just a low-value impulse buy

### What Session Data Could Not Show — and Where Frustration Evidence Came From

Mishipay's session logs only capture customers who reached a kiosk and initiated a session. Customers who saw the queue and left without buying generated no data. Evidencing their frustration required triangulating across sources external to the session database:

- MLSE retail staff observations via the Customer Success team, the original trigger for the investigation. Staff had directly observed fans turning away during intermissions when checkout queues became visible from the store entrance, documented in CS account notes
- Revenue-per-attendee gap against venue retail benchmarks, the NRF benchmarks sports venue merchandise at $8–12 per attendee annually. Comparing MLSE's actual revenue per game-day attendee against this figure provided a quantitative proxy for unconverted purchase intent, independent of Mishipay's data
- Session timing distribution within intermissions, session start timestamps showed a heavy concentration in the first 3–5 minutes of each window, with a sharp drop-off thereafter, consistent with a visible queue forming early and deterring later arrivals

## Problem Statement

### Formal Statement

At Scotiabank Arena's Real Sports Apparel stores, fans experience average checkout wait times of approximately 90 seconds at the counter, with additional queue times of 5–10 minutes during peak game-day windows (pre-game, intermissions, post-game). At a venue capacity of 19,000+ fans, even 1% purchase intent creates demand that conventional checkout infrastructure cannot absorb within a 15-minute intermission. The result: measurable purchase abandonment, constrained per-event revenue, and a fan experience that doesn't match the premium standard set by the venue.

### Why This Problem Matters at Scale

Scotiabank Arena hosts 82 home events per season, 41 Toronto Maple Leafs games and 41 Toronto Raptors games. Each event has at minimum two 15-minute intermissions: approximately 30 minutes of peak purchase opportunity per event. At 90 seconds per transaction across 3 checkout points, maximum throughput is ~30 customers per intermission. Reducing transaction time to 20 seconds increases theoretical throughput to ~135 customers, a 4.5× capacity increase on identical floor space and headcount. Multiplied across 82 events, the revenue compounding effect is significant.

### User Personas

#### Persona 1: The Game-Day Fan

- **Name:** Jordan, 28, season ticket holder, attends ~10 Maple Leafs games per year
- **Goal:** Buy a jersey before the second period starts; be back in his seat for the puck drop
- **Frustration:** Queue at Real Sports Apparel during intermission is regularly 10+ people deep, by the time he's served, the period has restarted and he's missed the opening minutes
- **Behaviour:** Abandons purchase if the queue appears longer than ~5 minutes; decides it's "not worth it" and forfeits the intended purchase entirely

> "I'd buy more merch if I didn't have to miss the game to get it," representative fan sentiment

#### Persona 2: MLSE Retail Operations

- **Name:** Mark Blanchard, Director of Retail, MLSE
- **Goal:** Maximise transaction throughput during compressed game-day peaks without increasing headcount per event
- **Frustration:** Can staff 2–3 cashiers per store; demand spikes during intermissions exceed capacity regardless of staffing level
- **Constraint:** Adding cashiers isn't a viable solution, labour cost per event is fixed; what scales is transaction speed, not headcount

> "We wanted fans spending time watching the action, not waiting in line."

### Options Considered

Before proposing RFID, I evaluated four distinct solutions against three criteria: checkout speed improvement, implementation complexity relative to Mishipay's existing stack, and operational cost impact for MLSE. The goal was to identify the option that addressed the core constraint — multi-item checkout speed — without introducing new friction or ongoing cost.

### Why RFID Was the Right Choice

Every other option required the fan to make a trade-off, "is this worth missing some of the game?" RFID eliminates the trade-off. At under 20 seconds from kiosk approach to receipt, the transaction fits entirely within the natural movement of a fan between the concourse and their seat. No mental calculation needed.

The product insight wasn't that RFID is technically faster. It's that RFID is the only option that makes buying merchandise compatible with being a fan at a live event. That distinction, solving for the fan experience, not the transaction metric, is what made the case compelling and what ultimately drove the revenue outcome. A critical de-risking insight: sports merchandise is already RFID-tagged by brands as standard practice for inventory management and loss prevention, so Mishipay's kiosk simply needed to read those tags and integrate with MLSE's existing POS. RFID's exit-gate tag deactivation also eliminated the need for staff to manually remove hard security tags after payment, removing the last remaining human intervention from the checkout journey.

### Scope

**In Scope — MVP**

- RFID bulk-scan kiosk replacing manned till checkout at Real Sports Apparel
- Payment: contactless card, chip, mobile wallet (Apple Pay / Google Pay)
- Custom jersey support: player selection, name and number input, inline in the same checkout flow
- RFID labels cleared in the Mishipay system; fan walks out without any further step or staff check
- Session logging and sales data integration with MLSE's POS and reporting systems
- Staff-assist mode: override / manual lookup for non-RFID-tagged items

**Out of Scope — Deferred**

- Mobile app checkout as an alternative flow (low adoption expected; deferred to post-MVP evaluation)
- Loyalty points accrual and redemption at the RFID kiosk (integration complexity; separate roadmap track)
- Returns and exchanges via kiosk (operational policy decision with MLSE; staffed process retained)
- Multi-language interface (English only for pilot; localisation deferred for stadium context)

## Product Requirements

### Core Features — MVP

- RFID scanning zone: reads all RFID-tagged items placed within the zone simultaneously in under 5 seconds
- Item confirmation screen: touchscreen display showing all detected items with images, names, and prices before payment
- Add / remove items: customer can return an item to the zone to remove it, or manually search for non-tagged items
- Payment flow: contactless, chip, and swipe; receipt by email or printed
- Staff management dashboard: view session status, handle exceptions, process manual overrides

### Edge Case: Custom Jerseys

Custom jerseys represent a minority of transactions by volume but a disproportionate share of revenue, they're high-value items fans specifically travel to purchase at the venue. The requirement was to support custom jersey checkout in the same RFID flow, with no separate queue or process. When a customisable jersey SKU is detected by the RFID reader, the kiosk displays a personalisation prompt (player name, number, font) before the payment step, and the customisation is submitted to the production queue on payment confirmation. This required close coordination between Mishipay's product team and MLSE's jersey fulfilment workflow to define the order data contract.

Multiple fans check out in parallel, and peak-period throughput scales with the number of kiosks rather than the number of cashiers.

## Pilot Management & Business Impact

### Pilot Design

The pilot was structured around a small number of regular-season home games at Scotiabank Arena in February 2024, deliberately chosen over playoff games to lower the stakes of a first deployment. Success criteria were defined upfront and shared with MLSE before the pilot began: average transaction time under 30 seconds, staff intervention rate under 20%, and zero critical failures during peak intermission periods.
